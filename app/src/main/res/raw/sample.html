One

The Last Crossing

The last ferry of the night left the harbour at 11:40, and on most nights it left almost empty. Mara Novak had piloted it for nine years. She knew the sound of every rope against the posts, the way the engine coughed twice before it settled, and the exact moment, halfway across the bay, when the lights of the town behind her grew smaller than the lights of the island ahead.

She liked the last crossing best. The day passengers were gone by then: the tourists with their cameras, the workers with their coffee, the children who pressed their faces against the windows and counted the gulls. At night there were only a few regulars, and they rarely spoke. A nurse going home after a long shift. Two fishermen who slept with their caps over their eyes. An old man who always sat in the same seat and read the same newspaper, folded into a careful square.

On this particular night, the wind had dropped and the water was flat and black, like a sheet of glass laid over something deep. Mara checked the radio, checked the fuel, and stepped out onto the small deck behind the wheelhouse to look at the sky. There were no stars. A low, soft cloud had come in from the sea, and the air smelled of salt and rain that had not yet decided to fall.

"Quiet tonight," said Tomas, the deckhand, coiling the last rope at her feet. He was nineteen and had worked on the ferry for a single summer, which in his opinion made him an expert on everything.

"It's always quiet," Mara said.

"Not like this." He looked out at the water. "It's too flat. My grandfather used to say that when the sea goes flat, it's listening."

"Your grandfather sold umbrellas."

"He was right about the weather, though. Every time."

She laughed, and went back inside, and pulled the horn twice. The ferry slid away from the harbour wall, and the town began, slowly, to shrink.

Two

The Passenger with the Clock

There was a new passenger that night. Mara noticed her only after they had cleared the harbour, when she walked through the cabin on her usual round. The woman sat at the very back, near the window, with a wooden box on her knees. She was perhaps sixty, with grey hair cut short and a coat that was too warm for the season. She held the box carefully, the way people hold something that is either very old or very fragile.

The box was ticking.

It was not a loud sound. In the daytime, with the engine and the children and the gulls, nobody would have heard it at all. But at night, in the empty cabin, the ticking was steady and clear, and the nurse looked up from her phone, and one of the fishermen lifted his cap to see where it came from, and then put it down again.

"It's a clock," the woman said, before Mara could ask. "I'm sorry. It doesn't stop."

"That's all right," said Mara. "Most of the things on this boat don't stop either."

The woman smiled at that. Up close, her eyes were very pale, almost the colour of the water in the morning. She introduced herself as Dr. Hana Brandt, and said that she was going to the island to return something that had been borrowed a long time ago.

"The clock?"

"The clock," said Dr. Brandt. "It belonged to the lighthouse. My father took it in 1962, when they replaced the old mechanism with an electric one. He was the last keeper. He said it was too good a clock to throw away, and that one day someone would want it back." She paused. "Nobody ever asked. But he made me promise, and promises are like clocks: they keep running, whether you look at them or not."

Three

Fog

The fog came in twenty minutes later, faster than anything Mara had seen in nine years. One moment the island lights were there, small and yellow and steady; the next, they were gone, and the windows of the wheelhouse showed nothing but a soft grey wall, pressed close against the glass.

She slowed the engine. She switched on the fog horn, which gave its long, low, mournful note every two minutes, and she watched the radar, where the island was a bright green shape and the ferry was a small dot moving carefully toward it. There was nothing to worry about. The radar did not care about fog. The channel was deep and wide, and she had crossed it more than six thousand times.

And then the radar went dark.

It did not flicker or fade. It simply stopped, as if someone had pulled a plug, and the screen became a flat black square. Mara tapped it, which she knew was useless, and then she tried the radio, and the radio gave her only a hiss, steady and meaningless, like the sound of the sea inside a shell.

Tomas appeared in the doorway. "The lights in the cabin went out," he said. "Just for a second. Then they came back. Is that normal?"

"No," said Mara.

She cut the engine to its slowest speed, and the ferry drifted forward through the grey. Without the radar, without the lights of the island, there was nothing to steer by. She knew the heading by heart, of course, but a heading is only a direction, not a place, and the tide in the channel was strong enough to carry a slow boat sideways onto the rocks below the old lighthouse.

Somewhere in the cabin, very clearly, the clock was ticking.

Four

What the Clock Counted

Dr. Brandt came up to the wheelhouse with the box held against her chest. She did not ask what had happened. She looked at the black radar screen, and at the fog, and then she set the box down on the chart table and opened it.

Inside was a brass clock, about the size of a dinner plate, with a white face and thin black hands. But it was not like any clock Mara had seen. Around the edge of the face, where the minutes should have been, there was a second ring of numbers, and a small window near the bottom where a disc slowly turned.

"The old light did not just turn," said Dr. Brandt. "It turned at a very particular speed. Every lighthouse has its own signature: so many flashes, so many seconds of dark. Ships knew where they were by counting. This clock drove the lens. My father said that if you ever lost your way in the fog, you only had to listen for the light, because the light and the clock were the same thing."

"The light is electric now," said Mara. "It doesn't need the clock."

"Yes," said Dr. Brandt. "But listen."

Mara listened. At first she heard only the engine, low and patient, and the water against the hull. Then, under it, she heard the clock: tick, tick, tick, and then a pause, longer than the others, and then again. Four ticks and a pause. Four ticks and a pause.

And far away, through the fog, so faint that at first she thought she had imagined it, there was an answer. A low hum, rising and falling, four times, and then silence. The electric light on the island did not make a sound, she was sure of that. But something out there was keeping time with the clock on her chart table, and it was coming from the left, from the north, from exactly where the rocks were not.

She turned the wheel, very gently, toward the sound.

Five

Morning

They came out of the fog eleven minutes later, as suddenly as they had gone into it. The island was right there, close enough to see the windows of the harbour office, and the lighthouse stood above it, turning its white beam over the water: four flashes, and a pause. Four flashes, and a pause.

The radar flickered and woke up. The radio coughed and began, apologetically, to work again. In the cabin, the lights were steady, and the nurse was asleep, and the fishermen had not moved at all.

Mara brought the ferry in against the wall with barely a bump. Tomas threw the ropes. Dr. Brandt closed the wooden box, and thanked them both, and walked up the hill toward the lighthouse with the clock held carefully against her chest. Mara watched her go until the road turned and the coat disappeared behind a stone wall.

"Did you hear it?" asked Tomas. "Out there, in the fog. The humming."

"I heard something," said Mara.

"My grandfather would have said that the sea was listening." He grinned. "And that the lighthouse answered."

"Your grandfather sold umbrellas," said Mara. But she said it softly, and she did not laugh this time.

The next morning, the harbour office called to say that the old clock had been returned to the lighthouse, and that it had been mounted on the wall of the lamp room, where it was still running. Nobody could explain how it kept such perfect time with a light that no longer needed it. Nobody could explain the fog either, or the radar, or the radio. The engineers came, and checked everything, and found nothing wrong.

Mara still pilots the last ferry of the night. On most nights it leaves almost empty, and the sea is just the sea. But sometimes, when the wind drops and the water goes flat and black, she cuts the engine for a moment in the middle of the bay, and listens. And sometimes, very faintly, from the north, she hears it: four, and a pause. Four, and a pause...

Then she starts the engine again, and takes them home.
